AI text is taking over the web's training data — that's a problem for model builders, not a verdict on your newsletter

A new study (arXiv 2609.40295, Sep 2026) finds AI's share of web text roughly tripled in two years. Training on that mix now costs up to 3x the compute of clean human text. That's a real warning about where LLM pretraining is headed. It is not a finding about whether AI-assisted newsletters are good or bad for readers — those are different questions. Keep using AI to draft. If anything changes, it's how you differentiate and disclose, not whether you use the tool.

Assumptions & open questions, up front

  • Figures below are as reported by the study (Pangram detector, post quality-filtering). We did not independently re-verify the paper's numbers.
  • "1.6x / 2.1x / 3.0x compute" means compute needed to match human-only training quality — not an absolute cost figure.
  • 42% and 51% AI-share are the study's forecasts, not observed data. Treat them as a projection, not a certainty.
  • The study measures general web text and pretraining economics. It does not measure newsletter trust or engagement — that connection is ours to reason through below.

  • Compute penalty scales with AI share: today's mix already costs 1.6x the compute of human-only text. Forecast shares push that to 2–3x.
  • Repeating beats adding: re-using the same human text for multiple epochs outperforms diluting the corpus with fresh AI text. Synthetic volume isn't a free substitute for human volume.
  • Not a guess — 800 trained models: this is an empirical scaling study, not an extrapolation from a handful of runs.

  • Filters select for AI-like traits, not against them: "quality" heuristics (fluency, structure, on-topic coherence) are exactly the traits AI writing has more consistently than average web text.
  • That makes it self-reinforcing: each filtering pass is reported to skew the surviving mix further toward AI text — part of why the share keeps climbing even after filtering.

  • Different problem, different fix: the paper is about pretraining data economics. It says nothing about whether AI-assisted drafting serves readers well.
  • Your real levers are editorial, not data-hygiene: fact-check what the AI drafts, keep a voice readers recognize, and be clear about your process if asked.
  • The one honest link: every AI-drafted newsletter does add to the web's AI-text share. One newsletter's contribution to a web-scale trend is negligible — worth knowing, not worth over-weighting.
